ROAS Is Not Profit: Break-Even ROAS for Your GCC Shopify Store

Last reviewed: 2026-10-28 · 4 min read

"My campaign has a 3.5 ROAS, so it's profitable." Sometimes true, often not. ROAS compares sales to ad spend; it says nothing about your product cost, VAT, payment fees, shipping, returns or discounts. Two products can show the identical ROAS and have opposite profit. This guide shows how to calculate the break-even ROAS for each product, with an illustrative example in SAR.

What ROAS does not know

ROAS = revenue attributed to ads ÷ ad spend. Spend SAR 10,000, see SAR 35,000 in attributed sales, and ROAS is 3.5. The number does not include:

  • product cost (COGS),
  • VAT, if the order value sent to the ad platform includes it,
  • payment fees, shipping and packaging,
  • returns and refused cash-on-delivery orders that happen after the sale is recorded,
  • discounts, if they are not passed through accurately.

The formula

First compute contribution before ads per order: revenue (after removing VAT) minus product cost, shipping, payment fees and packaging. Then:

Break-even ROAS = revenue ÷ contribution (equivalently 1 ÷ contribution margin)

It answers: how many riyals of sales do I need per riyal of ads just to break even?

Example (illustrative): price SAR 230 including 15% VAT, so revenue is 200 (ZATCA's e-commerce guideline uses 15/115 to extract VAT from inclusive prices). Costs: product 70, shipping 22, packaging 3, mada fee 5.03 (assumed 1.75% + SAR 1). Contribution = 200 − 100.03 = SAR 99.97, about 50% of revenue. Break-even ROAS = 200 ÷ 99.97 = 2.0 on a pre-VAT basis.

The VAT trap

If your ad platform records order value as the customer saw it (VAT-inclusive), compare it to a break-even computed on the inclusive price: 230 ÷ 99.97 = 2.30. If it records ex-VAT value, use 2.0. A 15% difference can flip a campaign from profit to loss without you noticing. Check what value your pixel or API actually sends before comparing.

Same ROAS, opposite results

Assume SAR 10,000 spend and SAR 35,000 of VAT-inclusive attributed sales (ROAS 3.5), roughly 152 orders at SAR 230. (Illustrative; no returns.)

Product A (cost 70) Product B (cost 120)
Contribution per order 99.97 49.97
Break-even ROAS (VAT-inclusive basis) 2.30 4.60
Contribution from 152 orders 15,214 7,605
Minus ad spend 10,000 +5,214 −2,395

Product A makes money at 3.5. Product B loses money at 3.5. If you judged both on ROAS alone, you might scale the loser.

A simpler cousin: break-even cost per order

Flip the formula: the most you can pay to acquire one order equals that order's contribution. In the example, A can cost up to SAR 99.97 per order, B up to SAR 49.97. If your campaigns deliver orders at SAR 65, A is profitable and B is not.

Returns raise the bar

If 10% of orders come back and you pay outbound and return shipping, average contribution falls. For Product A, assuming returned goods are resellable and the gateway fee is not refunded, it drops from SAR 99.97 to about SAR 84.78, which moves break-even from 2.30 to about 2.71 on the inclusive basis. Saudi consumers have a right to withdraw within seven days under Article 13 of the E-Commerce Law, with exceptions, and costs depend on your policy, so measure your actual return rate.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing a VAT-inclusive ROAS to a pre-VAT break-even (or the reverse).
  • Using one store-wide margin when products differ.
  • Forgetting that break-even on contribution does not cover fixed costs.
  • Judging a campaign before returns and refusals have landed.
  • Trusting the platform's number without reconciling it against your store orders.

FAQ

Is ROAS a bad metric? No. It is useful for comparing campaigns on the same product. It just does not decide profit by itself.

What about repeat customers? They can justify a lower first-order margin, but measure your actual repeat rate rather than assuming it.

What is a good ROAS? There is no universal number. A good ROAS is one that clears your product's break-even with a margin of safety.
